History

From Solo Agents to Agent Teams

LLM-based multi-agent systems went from a curiosity to a research field in under two years. Here is the road that led to a survey being needed at all.

Role-playing "AI User" and "AI Assistant" agents complete tasks through conversation — an early open framework for cooperative LLM agents.

A software company in prompts: product manager, architect, engineer. Encodes human SOPs to keep a role-based team coordinated.

Key Insight

LLM agents were never trained to collaborate. LLMs are standalone next-token predictors — yet researchers keep putting them in teams. What separates systems that gain collective intelligence from systems that just burn tokens is the collaboration mechanism: who talks to whom, why, under which rules, and who directs the traffic.

A COLLABORATION CHANNEL, PER THE SURVEY
channel c = actors + type + structure + strategy
Change any attribute and you get a different channel — a different team chemistry.
Chapter 01

The Problem with Ad-Hoc Teams

By 2024, every new multi-agent framework shipped its own collaboration recipe. The survey's diagnosis: the field was drowning in frameworks and starving for mechanisms.

🌀
Every Paper Invents Its Own Team
  • Each MAS framework ships an ad-hoc, one-off collaboration recipe
  • No shared vocabulary: "role", "channel", "protocol" mean different things
  • Prior surveys focus on single agents or applications — not mechanisms
  • Evaluations use narrow, differing setups — results are incomparable
  • Failure modes get re-discovered, not learned from, by every new builder
🗺️
The Survey's Solution: a Mechanism-Level Map
  • Characterize what happens between agents, not which framework they use
  • Five dimensions: actors, types, structures, strategies, coordination
  • One lens for CAMEL, MetaGPT, AutoGen, ChatDev, DyLAN, debate systems…
  • Lessons learned and open problems collected in one place
  • An extensible framework — new systems slot into the existing map
Analogy — A Playbook for Sports Teams

⚾ A soccer coach does not invent passing patterns from scratch before every match. They study cataloged formations — 4-4-2, 4-3-3, counter-attack — each with known strengths, weaknesses, and failure modes. This survey is the first playbook for LLM agent teams: you pick a topology, a strategy, and an interaction type the way a coach picks a formation — deliberately, knowing the trade-offs, instead of reinventing teamwork every game.

Chapter 02

The Survey's Map — Five Dimensions

The survey's unit of analysis is the collaboration channel between agents. Every channel is described along the same five dimensions — which makes wildly different systems finally comparable.

StrategyHow It WorksBest ForWatch Out
Rule-basedInteractions strictly controlled by predefined rules (debate turns, majority rule, peer review, event-triggered messaging)Consistency & fairness; predictable, debuggable workflowsLow adaptability — rule count explodes as tasks get complex

Dimension 5a — Static Orchestration

The wiring is fixed up front from domain knowledge. Sequential chaining — one agent's output feeds the next — beat single-agent chain-of-thought on college-level science MCQs. MapCoder hard-codes a recall → planning → coding → debugging pipeline; MACRec fixes Manager / Analyst / Reflector roles for recommendations.

Dimension 5b — Dynamic Orchestration

The team re-organizes itself mid-task. Solo Performance Prompting spins up persona-based agents per input; a DAG Orchestrator builds a task graph on the fly and a Delegator merges results. Team formation is optimizable too: DyLAN first runs Team Optimization (an LLM ranker deactivates weak agents, with early stopping), then solves the task with the survivors.

Chapter 03

Cooperate, Compete — or Both?

The survey's second dimension: WHY agents interact. Three collaboration types, plus the hybrid channels that mix them.

Type 1 — Cooperation
𝒪_collab = ⋃ oᵢ  (objectives unify)

Agents align individual goals with a shared one. Feedback loops (Actor → Evaluator → Self-Reflection), shared belief states, and specialist roles (MetaGPT's assembly line). + divides sub-tasks by strength. − one agent's failure can be amplified.

Type 2 — Competition
𝒪_collab = { oᵢ | oᵢ ≠ oⱼ }  (objectives clash)

Agents pursue rival goals — debate to persuade a judge, strategic gameplay in LLMARENA's seven dynamic game environments. + pushes reasoning, creativity, robustness. − needs mechanisms to resolve conflicts and keep rivalry beneficial.

Type 3 — Coopetition
cooperate on this ⊕ compete on that

A strategic blend: negotiation agents trade off conflicting interests to reach mutually beneficial agreements — and mixture-of-experts is coopetition too: expert models compete to contribute, a gating network picks the winners.

Hybrid Channels — Real Systems Mix Types

One system can run several channels at once. In LEGO, three agents cooperate to gather task information, then an Explainer and a Critic compete to refine the answer. In debate setups, two agents run a competitive channel while a judge holds cooperative channels with both.

a₁ ⇄ a₂  competitive channel ccomp (debate)  |  a₁ ⇄ a₃, a₂ ⇄ a₃  cooperative channels (verdict)

🧍 Single agent
Cheapest and fastest — but nothing catches a systematic error. Use when tasks are simple or cost-sensitive.
💬 Debate
Reasoning gets exposed and checked round by round — the survey cites debate as a boost to factuality and reasoning. Costs N× agents × rounds.
🗳️ Majority vote
Cheap parallel opinions — but the survey warns voting fails when agents share the same bias, aggregating LLM overconfidence instead of fixing it.
Chapter 04

How Agents Wire Up

The survey's third dimension: communication structures. Three topologies, each with its own failure personality — and the survey stresses the optimal structure varies with the task and the agent mix.

Dimension 3 — Communication Structures (from the survey's Table 4)
StructureDefinitionStrengthsWeaknesses
Centralized (star)Every agent connects to one hub agent that manages, controls, and coordinates all interactions (LLM-Blender, AgentCoord; federated learning as a centralized MAS)Simple to design; efficient resource allocationIf the central node fails, the entire system might collapse
Decentralized (peer-to-peer)Decision-making is distributed; agents talk directly to each other (debate systems, MedAgent, ChatDev, generative agent societies)Keeps working when agents fail; high scalability; autonomous agentsInefficient resource allocation; high communication overhead
HierarchicalAgents arranged in layers with distinct roles and levels of authority (DyLAN's multi-layer network, AgentVerse's hydrogen-siting team)Low bottleneck — tasks spread across levels; efficient allocationEdge agents become critical; high complexity and latency

One more survey gem: Yin et al. map four communication paradigms to four hardware-style topologies — memory → bus, report → star, relay → ring, debate → tree.

Chapter 05

When Teams Help — and When They Hurt

The survey's most practical lesson: a multi-agent system is not automatically smarter. Mechanism design decides whether the team wins or just multiplies the bill.

📈
What a Well-Designed Team Buys You
  • Distributed knowledge: agents retain and share diverse knowledge without overloading one model
  • Long-horizon planning: sub-tasks delegated across agents over extended interactions
  • Generalization: pooled expertise and personas cover more problem types
  • Efficiency: specialized agents work sub-tasks in parallel
  • Error-catching: debate and critic channels expose flawed reasoning
💸
What a Badly-Designed Team Costs You
  • Coordination overhead: frequent multi-channel communication inflates cost and complexity
  • Error propagation: one failing agent drags the whole system down
  • Suboptimal channels: a poorly designed MAS can lose to one strong agent with a good prompt
  • Unpredictable behavior: agents messaging themselves, or pretending to be clients
  • Scalability walls: coordination gets harder as the agent count grows
Failure Modes the Survey Catalogs
  • Cascading hallucinations — a single agent's hallucination is spread and reinforced by others, escalating minor inaccuracies into critical failures
  • Overconfidence aggregation — LLMs persistently assert wrong outputs; voting over confident-but-wrong agents just amplifies the error
  • Infinite conversation loops and amplified hallucination in open-ended cooperative chat
  • Exploitation — in competitive settings, agents can game the system; compromised agents can be manipulated into harmful behavior
  • Structural fragility — hub failure (centralized), branch loss (hierarchical), or message storms (decentralized)

What This Survey Does NOT Solve

It is a map, not a benchmark. The survey organizes mechanisms but runs no head-to-head experiments quantifying which mechanism wins on which task — it explicitly calls out the absence of standardized MAS evaluation protocols as an open problem. It also does not train collaborative LLMs: models are still "standalone algorithms" not specifically trained for teamwork. And its applications review (5G/6G, Industry 5.0, QA/NLG, social simulation) stays qualitative — the numbers-heavy comparisons are left to the benchmarks it inspires.

Legacy

Impact — From Frameworks to a Science of Teams

By mapping mechanisms instead of listing frameworks, the survey gives the field a shared foundation — and a to-do list.

🗺️ A shared vocabulary
Five dimensions that describe any LLM agent team — the first common language for comparing CAMEL to AutoGen to DyLAN on the same axes.
🧭 Design guidance
Pick mechanisms like a coach picks formations: rule-based for procedure-heavy tasks, role-based for specialization, model-based for uncertainty, debate for verification.
📊 Benchmark design
Flagged the missing standardized MAS evaluation. Weeks later, MultiAgentBench (Mar 2025) arrived, purpose-built for collaboration and competition.
💰 A cost-benefit lens
Teams are not free: coordination costs, communication overhead, and error propagation must be weighed against parallelism and error-catching.
🔓 An open-problems list
The road to artificial collective intelligence: unified governance, richer shared decision-making, MAS scaling laws, and safety against hallucination cascades.
🧩 Mechanisms over frameworks
The field's shift in focus: describe what agents DO between each other, not which toolkit they're built in — making lessons transferable across frameworks.
Deep Dive

Cooperation, Competition, Coopetition

The survey's map of 167 works runs along five dimensions — actors, types, structures, strategies, coordination. Its most practically useful finding is a warning dressed as a taxonomy: teams are not automatically better. A well-designed channel structure beats a single strong agent; a suboptimal multi-agent system loses to one good model with a good prompt.

🧭
The Design Space, Named
  • Three types — cooperation (aligned goals), competition (clashing), coopetition (both at once — and real systems mix them)
  • Three strategies — rule-based (predictable), role-based (SOPs à la MetaGPT), model-based (probabilistic, Theory-of-Mind)
  • Three topologies — star (centralized), decentralized mesh, hierarchical layers
  • Five dimensions total: actors · types · structures · strategies · coordination — a coordinate system, not a leaderboard
⚠️
The Warning and the Open Problems
  • More agents ≠ more capability — channel design decides whether communication helps or drowns
  • Centralized star: efficient, but the hub is a single point of failure
  • Decentralized: resilient, but coordination overhead grows with the mesh
  • Open problems: standardized MAS benchmarking, and safety against cascading hallucinations, overconfidence aggregation, adversarial exploitation
Interactive Demo — Topology Blast Radius

The same task on three topologies. Watch messages route through each structure — then press the failure button: one node goes rogue (hallucinating or adversarial). The blast radius is the topology's safety property, visible in one click.

VERDICT
Pick the topology for its failures, not its flowchart
Cascading hallucination and adversarial exploitation propagate along communication edges — topology is a safety parameter before it is an efficiency parameter. The survey's systems to compare against the demo: CAMEL (two-node mesh, all the classic failures), MetaGPT (role-based strategy, hierarchical), Magentic-One (star around an orchestrator), measured by MultiAgentBench.
🤝 Coopetition is the real default
Werewolf negotiation, marketplace agents, shared-resource coding teams — aligned and opposed goals co-exist in most real deployments. The survey treats it as first-class, not an edge case.
🧠 Model-based strategy
Agents that model each other's beliefs — Theory of Mind — coordinate better in competition. The strategy axis is really a theory-of-other-agents axis.
📉 Overconfidence aggregation
A team of confident agents voting produces confident wrong answers. Ensembling probabilities without calibration control amplifies shared bias — the survey's least-cited, most-important warning.
📏 The benchmarking gap
Before MultiAgentBench, MAS papers rarely shared environments — claims were incommensurable, exactly HELM's pre-protocol complaint in a new domain.

Key Takeaways

Everything you need to remember about this survey.

✅ The survey (Tran et al., arXiv 2501.06322) reviews 167 works and maps collaboration along five dimensions: actors, types, structures, strategies, coordination.
✅ Three collaboration types: cooperation (aligned goals), competition (clashing goals), coopetition (both at once) — and real systems mix them in hybrid channels.
✅ Three strategies: rule-based (predictable), role-based (SOPs and specialization, à la MetaGPT), model-based (probabilistic, Theory-of-Mind).
✅ Three topologies: centralized/star (hub = single point of failure), decentralized (resilient, high overhead), hierarchical (layered authority).
✅ Teams aren't automatically better: well-designed channels beat single agents (AutoGen), but suboptimal MAS loses to one strong agent with a good prompt.
✅ Biggest open problems: standardized MAS benchmarking, and safety against cascading hallucinations, overconfidence aggregation, and adversarial exploitation.
